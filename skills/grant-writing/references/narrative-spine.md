# The grant narrative spine

Each element with the question it must answer and the failure to avoid.

## Problem
Question: why does this matter, and why now?
Do: open on the concrete stakes. A reader should feel the problem in the first paragraph.
Avoid: a chronological literature review. Reviewers do not need the history, they need the point.

## Gap
Question: what specifically is missing, unknown, or unsolved?
Do: state it in one sentence a tired reviewer can repeat to a panel. The gap is the hinge the
whole proposal turns on.
Avoid: a vague "little is known about". Name the exact thing.

## Approach
Question: what is your idea, and why are you and now the right combination?
Do: lead with the insight or method that makes this newly possible. This is the pitch, not the
methods section. Signal novelty plainly.
Avoid: burying the idea inside procedure.

## Aims
Question: what will you actually do, and can it be done with this money in this time?
Do: two or three testable aims, each standing on its own. Size them to the budget. State the
expected outcome and what it would mean.
Avoid: aims that domino (Aim 2 needs Aim 1 to succeed), and aims that are really one aim split
in three, or five aims crammed into one grant.

## Impact
Question: what changes if this works?
Do: name the consequence for the field, and the wider or translational consequence if there is
one. Tie it back to the funder's priorities.
Avoid: grand claims with no mechanism connecting the work to the outcome.

## Feasibility and risk
Question: why should the panel believe you can deliver?
Do: preliminary data that de-risks the central bet, relevant track record, named collaborators
with letters, and the environment and infrastructure already in place. For each real risk, an
honest mitigation or alternative.
Avoid: pretending there are no risks, or listing risks with no plan. Both read badly.
