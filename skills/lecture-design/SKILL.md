# Lecture Design

Design for a room that checks out after ninety seconds and dips in and out for the rest. The
job is not to cover the material. It is to make one idea stick with people who are only half
listening.

## One load-bearing idea

Every talk has a single sentence the audience should be able to say afterward. Write it down
before you make a slide. Everything in the talk either serves that sentence or gets cut. A
talk that tries to land five ideas lands none.

## Open with the payoff

State the point in the first minute. Do not build to it. Academic instinct is to lay
groundwork and reveal the result at the end; a distracted audience has left by then. Give the
"so what" first, then earn it. Inverted pyramid, not a mystery novel.

## Budget the attention

Attention decays and resets. Plan the talk in arcs of about ten minutes, each ending in a
reset: a question, a demo, a concrete example, a change of pace or medium. Front-load what
matters most, because the back half will lose people no matter how good it is.

## Signpost

Show the shape of the talk once, early, then leave lightweight markers so someone who tuned
out for two minutes can find their place again. A running anchor ("we are on the second of
three problems") costs nothing and rescues the half-present.

## Show, do not tell

One figure, diagram, or live demo that carries the idea beats three bullets describing it.
If the point is a relationship, a process, or a comparison, draw it. Reach for a concrete
example over an abstract statement every time.

## Slides

The slide is a visual aid, not your speaker notes. The pre-talk checklist is in
[references/slide-checklist.md](references/slide-checklist.md). The core rules: one idea per
slide, a title that states the takeaway rather than names the topic (assertion-evidence, so
"CYP2C19 poor metabolizers cleared the drug slower" not "Results"), big readable type, high
contrast, and no wall of bullets. If you would read a slide aloud word for word, it is a
document, not a slide.

## Pace and delivery

Fewer slides than you think. Leave whitespace and silence. Rehearse the open and the close
until they are automatic, since those are what people remember. Time-box and finish early;
nobody has ever complained that a talk ended with time to spare.

## Close

Restate the one idea in the same words you opened with. Give a single clear takeaway or next
step. Do not end on a "Questions?" or "Thank you" slide; end on the sentence you want them to
carry out of the room, and leave that on screen while they ask questions.

## Adapting the format

A lecture can afford more repetition and checks for understanding than a conference talk. A
conference talk is shorter and needs a sharper single hook. A webinar loses the room fastest
of all, so lean hardest on early payoff, frequent resets, and interaction.
